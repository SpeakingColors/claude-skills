# DJ Gig Guide

Research date: 2026-10-23. Gear, prices, laws and visa rules change. Re-check anything that matters before a booking.

## 1. How to use this guide and the label system

- Read the label on each claim before you rely on it.
- VERIFIED (S#): read on a fetched page. The number points to the Sources list in section 10.
- COMMUNITY: forum, blog, law firm blog or user report. Useful, but not official.
- UNVERIFIED: not checked in this research. Treat as a lead, never as fact.
- An unlabelled tip in sections 3 and 9 is general practice advice, not a factual claim.
- Assistants: never state an UNVERIFIED item as fact. Say it is unchecked and suggest where to confirm it.
- Beginners: when money, law, visas or customs are involved, ask the official body or a professional.

## 2. DJ gear families

### 2.1 Controllers
- All-in-one controller: decks, mixer and sound card in one box, plugs into a laptop (S29 describes controllers as devices that mimic two turntables and a mixer).
- 2 channel: cheapest and smallest; fine for learning and small gigs.
- 4 channel: more inputs for extra decks, mics or external gear; bigger and heavier.
- Many controllers have a built-in sound card, so headphone cueing works without extra gear (S29).
- Current examples listed on AlphaTheta's site (S28): DDJ-FLX2, DDJ-GRV6. Check the maker's page for the rest of the range.

### 2.2 Standalone systems
- Standalone means no laptop: music plays from USB or SD, with the software built in.
- Engine DJ OS runs on standalone hardware; Engine DJ Desktop is for library prep (S12).
- Denon DJ lists Prime 4 G2, Prime GO+ and SC Live 4 as standalone systems (S27).
- AlphaTheta lists XDJ-AZ and XDJ-AN (S28). Older XDJ-RX style units: UNVERIFIED current status.

### 2.3 Club standard players and mixers
- CDJ is Pioneer DJ's player line; XDJ is the all-in-one or USB-first line (S10, S25).
- History (S10): CDJ-1000 (2001) became a club staple; CDJ-2000 (2009) added Pro DJ Link; CDJ-2000 Nexus (2012) and CDJ-2000NXS2 (2016) are common in clubs; CDJ-3000 (2020) was the first flagship without a CD drive; CDJ-3000X (2025) and CDJ-1500X (2026) are newer.
- Pioneer DJ Corporation changed its company name to AlphaTheta Corporation from 1 January 2020; the Pioneer DJ brand name was not affected, and since 23 January 2024 new products can carry the AlphaTheta brand next to Pioneer DJ (S25).
- AlphaTheta current list (S28): CDJ-1500X, CDJ-3000X, XDJ-AZ, XDJ-AN, DJM-V5, OMNIS-DUO.
- Discontinued or older (S10): CDJ-1000 family, CDJ-800 family, CDJ-900, CDJ-2000. Older ones still appear in clubs, so learn them.
- DJM mixers: DJM-900 series are common club mixers: COMMUNITY. Exact discontinued status of each: UNVERIFIED.
- Denon DJ Prime (SC5000 style players, X1800 mixer): COMMUNITY that they are used in some clubs; check the venue.
- Rane, Numark, Reloop, Native Instruments Traktor hardware: product lines exist; current model lists UNVERIFIED (not fetched). Check each maker's page.
- Engine DJ hardware seen on the Engine DJ page: Denon DJ and Rane (S12).

### 2.4 Turntables, vinyl and DVS (timecode)
- Direct-drive turntables are preferred for DJing; belt-drive motors can be damaged by DJ handling (S29).
- DVS: a special timecode record carries a signal; software reads speed, direction and position and plays your digital file (S9).
- Needs an audio interface or sound card, and there is always a small delay (lower is better) (S9).
- Examples named in S9: Serato (Scratch Live, Serato DJ), Traktor Scratch, Mixxx.
- Vinyl tips (COMMUNITY): carry spare needles and slipmats, clean records before play, check the venue turntable pitch and tonearm weight in soundcheck.

### 2.5 Headphones, booth monitors, cables, stands
- Closed-back headphones suit loud rooms; a detachable cable is easy to replace (S29).
- Bring your own headphones even if the venue has gear (COMMUNITY).
- Booth monitors: speakers aimed at the DJ. Ask for two (S18 example rider lists two pairs).
- Cables (COMMUNITY): carry spare USB cables, RCA to RCA, RCA to jack and jack to XLR, plus a power strip.
- Laptop stand: lifts the laptop above the mixer for space and cooling. Bring a small folding one.

### 2.6 USB sticks, formats, library export
- FAT32: single files up to 4 GB; exFAT lifts that limit and works on Windows, macOS and Linux (S24).
- Some older or embedded players may only read FAT32 (S24 says FAT32 remains a fallback for older devices). Check your player manual.
- Library export: rekordbox, Engine DJ and Serato each prepare their own USB format. Traktor export to club players: UNVERIFIED.
- Prepare a USB in the format of the club player (rekordbox for Pioneer players, Engine DJ for Denon): COMMUNITY.

### 2.7 What clubs usually provide vs what to bring
- Usually provided (COMMUNITY): mixer, two to four players or turntables, booth monitors, power and main PA.
- Usually brought by you: USB sticks (two copies), headphones, cables, laptop, controller if needed.
- Always ask and confirm which models are in the booth (S18 notes riders name models so the promoter can supply compatible gear).

## 3. Gig technique
- Soundcheck: arrive early, test every source (each player, laptop, mic), then play loud parts of your own music, not silence.
- Line check: play each channel, one at a time, and look at the meters on the mixer.
- Gain staging: set each channel gain so peaks sit just under the red; use channel faders for blending, not for loudness.
- Avoid clipping: if the master meter hits red, turn the gain down, not the master up. Distortion hurts the system and the crowd.
- Headphone cueing: check the next track privately, then match tempo and beats (S29). Keep the cue level safe for your ears.
- Feedback: caused by a mic or speaker loop. Turn the offending mic down, move it away from monitors, tell the sound person.
- Hum and ground loops: two devices grounded by their power cords and linked by a signal cable can form a loop and cause hum (S22).
  - Fixes (S22): balanced connections, isolation transformers or isolators. Plugging into one outlet does not always fix it.
  - Never remove or lift the safety earth plug (S22 says it is dangerous).
- Booth vs main level: the booth sound is not what the crowd hears. Ask the sound person how it sounds in the room and trust a second opinion.
- Set structure (COMMUNITY): open easy for the room, build, peak, then land. Keep track IDs for the set in a note.
- Reading the room: watch the dance floor, not your screen. Change direction if it empties.
- Requests: be polite, say yes only if it fits; a clear "not in this set" is fine.
- Handover and back to back: agree the first track, key and tempo before the swap; keep the master level equal; tell the next DJ the booth is theirs.
- Time slot discipline: end on time. Overrunning hurts the next DJ and your reputation.
- Backup plan: second USB, a phone or laptop with a cable for emergency playback, and a pre-made playlist.
- USB hygiene: label sticks, eject safely before pulling, keep a copy of the library, scan stick on your own machine before plugging into unknown computers.
- Recording the set: record from the mixer's record out or a USB. Note that recording and sharing can need licences (section 5).
- Live streaming basics: use a stable wired connection, test audio levels, check platform rules for DJ sets before going live (section 5.5).

## 4. Riders
### 4.1 What they are
- Technical rider: what the venue must provide for the performance, such as equipment, monitors, PA, soundcheck time (S19).
- Hospitality rider: comfort items such as food, drink, dressing room, transport, lodging (S19).
- A rider is a starting point, not a demand: venues can negotiate or decline (S19).

### 4.2 DJ tech rider template (copy and adapt)
- Header: DJ name, contact for tech questions, version date, expiry date (S19 suggests an expiry date and contact details).
- Must have (3 to 5 items only): list items you cannot perform without (S19 advice).
- Players: [two or four] [player models or equal], same model on all decks, firmware up to date, linked if possible.
- Mixer: [mixer model or equal], 4 channels, working EQ, crossfader and headphone cue.
- Turntables (if used): two direct-drive turntables with working pitch, cartridges and slipmats.
- Booth monitors: two, at DJ ear height, separate level control (S18 shows two pairs in an example).
- Power: stable mains with spare sockets within reach of the booth.
- Booth: stable table, enough space for a laptop, clear of drinks, a light.
- Own gear: say if you bring a controller or laptop and what cables the venue mixer needs (S18).
- Soundcheck: [minutes] before doors, with access to the booth.
- Alternatives: add "or equal" to models so the venue can say yes (S19).
- Hospitality (for travel gigs): meals or a meal buyout (S19 mentions $15 to $30 per person as a common buyout), drinks, a place to change, local transport, hotel if travelling.

### 4.3 How to ask politely and what promoters accept
- Send the rider when you confirm the booking, before signing, so changes can be agreed (S19).
- Keep it short: about 1 to 3 pages technical and 1 to 2 pages hospitality (S19).
- Match it to your level; emerging artists should avoid luxury items (S19).
- Be specific, flexible on non-essentials, and confirm any agreed changes in writing (S19).
- Keep alcohol requests modest (S19).
- Small venues cannot match festivals; use tiered or "essential" markings (S19).
- Sample wording (UNVERIFIED as an industry standard): "Thanks for the booking. Attached is my tech rider; the items marked ESSENTIAL are the only ones I really need. Happy to talk about alternatives."

## 5. Contracts and money
NOT LEGAL ADVICE. Laws differ by country. Ask a local lawyer, or a union or musicians' or DJ association, before signing or when in doubt.

### 5.1 What a booking agreement usually contains (COMMUNITY unless noted)
- Date, venue, set times, set length.
- Fee, currency and how it is paid.
- Deposit and the date the rest is due.
- Cancellation terms for both sides, and force majeure (events outside anyone's control).
- Travel, hotel, transport and meals: who pays and who books.
- Rider (technical and hospitality attached).
- Exclusivity or radius clause: stops you from playing nearby events for a time window.
- Payment terms, invoicing and tax or withholding (varies by country: UNVERIFIED).
- Contacts and an on-the-day tech contact.

### 5.2 Red flags (COMMUNITY)
- No written agreement, only chat messages with no clear fee.
- Pay "after the door count" with no minimum.
- Very wide exclusivity radius or long time window.
- One-sided cancellation: the venue can cancel free, you pay a penalty.
- Asking you to pay to play, or to bring a guaranteed crowd.
- Pressure to skip the deposit "this once".

### 5.3 Get it in writing
- Confirm key terms in an email even if the venue is informal.
- Agree deposits and kill fees (a payment owed if the other side cancels late) before you book travel.
- Keep copies of contracts, invoices and receipts.

### 5.4 Copyright, neighbouring rights and music licensing basics
- A performing rights organisation (PRO) collects royalties for public performance of music on behalf of rights holders (S8).
- Venues and organisations that use music publicly usually pay the PRO for a licence (S8). This means the venue licence often covers background and live music; confirm with the venue (S8 does not cover each country).
- A PRO licence typically covers all works in that PRO's repertoire rather than one by one (S8).
- Related (neighbouring) rights belong to performers and record producers, and are separate from authors' copyright (S23). A recorded song can carry four rights: composer, lyricist, performers, producer (S23).
- Performers' rights are separate from "performing rights" paid to composers (S23).
- US law does not explicitly recognise performers' rights; protection relies on state law (S23).
- PRS for Music licenses music use and collects royalties for its members (S16); what it requires of venues or DJs was not confirmed.
- Who pays for what at a particular gig (venue, promoter or DJ): UNVERIFIED; ask the venue if its licence is in place.

### 5.5 DJ mix recording and streaming
- A recorded mix is a new use of other people's music; rights and licences differ by service and country.
- Mixcloud: its licence covers streaming only, not downloads; uploaders should tag tracks with accurate artist and song data (S32, Wikipedia summary; Mixcloud's own legal page could not be fetched).
- Other platforms' DJ set rules (YouTube, Twitch, SoundCloud): UNVERIFIED. Read the platform terms before going live.
- Remix, mashup and sampling clearance: UNVERIFIED; ask the collecting society or a lawyer.

## 7. Travel with gear
### 7.1 Flights and batteries (S2, S3)
- Spare lithium batteries and power banks go in carry-on, never checked bags (S2, S3).
- Protect terminals: tape, a case or original packaging (S2).
- FAA (S2): lithium-ion spares up to 100 Wh are allowed without approval; 101 to 160 Wh need airline approval, with a limit of two spares; larger is not covered.
- Wh = volts x amp-hours (S2).
- Damaged or recalled batteries must not fly (S2).
- IATA says allowances depend on Wh rating or lithium content and that spares are not allowed in checked baggage (S3). Airline rules can be stricter (S2).
- EASA page: not fetched; UNVERIFIED. Check your airline and your departure country.

### 7.2 Cases, insurance, customs, laptop, backups
- Flight cases (COMMUNITY): protect controllers and players; check size and weight limits with the airline before packing.
- Insurance (UNVERIFIED): check gear cover for travel, theft and checked baggage; read the exclusions.
- ATA carnet: a "passport for goods" that lets goods enter other countries temporarily without duties or taxes (S5, S7).
  - Valid up to one year, accepted in about 80 countries and territories, issued through a national guaranteeing association (S5, S7).
  - Designed for professional equipment and similar goods (S5); S7 lists artists and technicians among users. S5 does not mention musical instruments.
  - Which DJ gear counts, and fees: UNVERIFIED; ask the national chamber of commerce.
- Laptop (COMMUNITY): carry it in the cabin, update and back up before travel, bring a spare charger.
- Backups (COMMUNITY): two USB copies in separate bags and a cloud copy of your library.
- Power adapters: see section 6 and S26; carry a universal adapter plus a small power strip.

## 8. Hearing health
- NIOSH recommends keeping noise exposure to 85 dBA averaged over an 8-hour workday (S1, S30, S31).
- For every 3 dBA increase, NIOSH recommends halving the exposure time (S30, S31). The exact level and time table is in another NIOSH document (S30).
- Repeated exposure at 85 dBA or higher can cause permanent hearing loss (S21).
- Hearing protection should be used when noise cannot be reduced and levels exceed 85 dBA (S30).
- NIOSH musician recommendations include education, annual hearing checks, sound-level checks, hearing protection and custom earplugs (S31, Wikipedia summary).
- Musician earplugs reduce volume while keeping sound quality (S31). Fit and brand: ask an audiologist.
- Tips (COMMUNITY): take quiet breaks, keep monitor levels low, get a baseline hearing test, see a doctor if you get ringing (tinnitus) (S1 also advises a test if hearing changes).

## 9. Checklists (one page)
### Before the gig
- [ ] Contract, fee, set time and length confirmed in writing
- [ ] Rider sent and answers received
- [ ] Gear models at the venue confirmed
- [ ] Music prepared and exported for the venue format
- [ ] Two USB sticks, spare cables, headphones, adapter
- [ ] Laptop and controller charged, backup copy made
- [ ] Travel, parking and load-in time planned
- [ ] Earplugs packed

### At the venue
- [ ] Meet the sound person and the previous DJ
- [ ] Check booth, power, monitors, mixer settings
- [ ] Soundcheck and line check; set gains below clipping
- [ ] Test every USB and cable you will use
- [ ] Agree handover and end time
- [ ] Start recording if you want one

### After the gig
- [ ] Take your gear, sticks and cables; check the booth
- [ ] Collect or confirm payment, get a receipt or send an invoice
- [ ] Back up the recording and tracklist
- [ ] Thank the promoter, note what worked
- [ ] Rest your ears
